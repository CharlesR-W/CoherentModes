Take a block, cut its snippets in two with the block's own averaged walk, redo the whole analysis on each half, and repeat. Each half is treated as a new probe: its cells are rebuilt on those snippets alone. The tree shows where the walk keeps finding a cheap cut and where it stops.

How a node is cut. A cut divides the node's snippets into two sides. For the node's snippets alone, I rebuild each cell of the block (centred over those snippets, {{fact:k}} nearest neighbours among them), average the walks, and try every threshold on each of the averaged walk's three slowest functions $\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3$. The cut kept is the one with the lowest conductance $\phi^*$: the share of walk steps that cross it, relative to the smaller side. Both sides need at least $\max(16, 5\%\ \text{of}\ n)$ snippets. SEBA with $r = 2$, run on the same walk, is shown beside the cut but never makes it. Nodes are split to depth 3, or until fewer than 64 snippets remain.

How a split is read. Compare the cut's cost with how fast the walk mixes otherwise. $\mu_2$ is the walk's second-slowest rate of mixing; the clustering ratio $\Upsilon = \mu_2 / \phi^*$ is large when the cut is much cheaper than anything else the walk offers, as for two groups with few links between them. I draw splits with $\Upsilon \ge {{fact:threshold}}$ as clear and the rest as weak (lighter, dashed outline; still clickable). The threshold was fixed before any real tree was opened, on synthetic data where the answer is known: planted groups gave $\Upsilon \ge$ {{fact:ups.planted.min}}, pure noise {{fact:ups.structureless.range}}. Those are reference points, not a test: the noise was isotropic and 32-dimensional, and $\Upsilon$ for structureless data depends on the geometry. $\Upsilon$ changes only how a split is drawn, never whether the recursion continues.
