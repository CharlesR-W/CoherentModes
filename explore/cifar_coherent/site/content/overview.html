I gave a ResNet-20 the same {{fact:n_images}} CIFAR-10 test images at {{fact:n_checkpoints}} checkpoints of its training and recorded its activations at {{fact:n_sites}} sites, from the first convolution to the logits. Then I asked which groups of images it treats alike, where in the network, and since when, without telling the method anything about labels. On this network, the last layers sort the images into four groups that line up with unions of classes, site b9 already splits vehicle-like from animal-like images by step {{fact:q.va.b9.half_step}}, and the grouping changes most between b2 and b3. This page gives the procedure, Neural Coherent Sets, and a small vocabulary for such statements; the explorer is where you check them.

Why coherent sets

This grew out of an observation by Stefan Heimersheim and collaborators about activation cells: interpolate between three real activations of a layer, and the network's response jumps sharply rather than blending, as if activation space were tiled into Voronoi-like cells. I spent a long time trying to decompile language models with cells, but cells resist direct analysis: the space is huge, the data only sample it, and nothing measures a cell's boundary cheaply.

My collaborator Brianna Grado-White and I make them tractable with a hypothesis: within a cell, the network propagates the labels of the training points inside it to the rest of the cell, a limited form of label propagation. A cell is then a cluster of points the network treats the same: a group that few neighbourhood links leave, which is what spectral clustering finds. Cells that persist across layers or training are coherent sets, and SEBA reads them off. I call the construction Neural Coherent Sets.

It also sits in a lineage of program reconstruction. Inferring weighted word or tree automata from Hankel matrices, which pair prefixes with continuations, is a form of program reconstruction, and the ambition is to reach richer programs, up to Turing machines. Dynamical systems have their own Hankel and Koopman methods, and Pughe-Sanford et al. (2025) suggest that coherent sets of a Koopman operator are a good lens on what neurons compute. Here depth or training plays the part of time, and the site averages the layers' walks rather than forming a Hankel-like product (the two agree closely).

The precise statements, and where the analogy stops
  • The hypothesis, stated precisely. Call a cell a region of a layer's activation space where the rest of the network computes nearly the same thing. If most neighbourhood edges of the walk $P$ stay inside cells, any downstream function $F$ satisfies $F \approx P F$ (harmonic, as in label propagation; Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty 2003) except at images with edges across a boundary, so its penalty $\tfrac12 \sum_{ij} P_{ij} \lVert F_i - F_j \rVert^2$ comes only from boundary-crossing edges. Cells are then low-cut sets, and the slowest eigenvectors approximately span their indicators; persistence across a block makes them coherent sets. This is a motivating hypothesis, not a result.
  • Hankel matrices. For a function $f$ on strings, $H_f[u, v] = f(uv)$, with prefixes $u$ as rows and suffixes $v$ as columns. Over the reals, $f$ is computed by a weighted finite automaton exactly when $H_f$ has finite rank, and $\operatorname{rank} H_f$ is the smallest linear state dimension of such an automaton. Reconstruction factors a rank-complete prefix–suffix block of $H_f$, together with its symbol-shifted blocks and boundary vectors, and yields a minimal realization up to a change of basis, not privileged hidden states (Balle et al. 2014). For a language, the distinct rows of its 0/1 Hankel matrix are the states of its minimal deterministic automaton (Myhill–Nerode). Hankel rank does not in general give the minimal number of hidden states of an HMM (positivity constraints apply); related spectral methods, under rank and nondegeneracy assumptions, learn observable representations of hidden Markov models (Hsu, Kakade and Zhang 2012).
  • The analogy. Read layer $a$ as the end of a prefix and layers $a$ to $b$ as its continuation. In the product $Q = M_b \cdots M_a$, image $j$'s column is where its layer-$a$ neighbourhood spreads as it diffuses through the later layers' neighbourhoods; images with similar columns are treated alike downstream, and $Q$'s leading singular vectors play the part of a small state. This is an analogy only: they inherit none of the Hankel reconstruction guarantees, the network is not an automaton, there is one fixed continuation rather than all suffixes, the walks measure neighbourhoods rather than outputs, and no rank here counts states.
  • The variational meaning. For one walk $P$ and the indicator of a set $A$, the penalty $\tfrac12 \sum_{ij} P_{ij} (f_i - f_j)^2$ equals $\operatorname{cut}(A, \bar A) = \sum_{i \in A,\, j \notin A} P_{ij}$, the total transition weight crossing between $A$ and its complement. Sinkhorn makes every image's degree 1, so normalised cut equals ratio cut. Relaxing the balanced two-valued indicator to a real function gives $\min_{f \perp \mathbf 1,\, \lVert f \rVert = 1} f^\top (I - P) f$, solved by the leading non-constant eigenvector of $P$ (equivalently of $M$) (von Luxburg 2007). The penalty is linear in the walk, so for a block the cut is averaged over its snapshots. The multiway relaxation takes orthonormal columns $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{r-1}$; SEBA turns that relaxed span into candidate hard sets and does not solve the discrete multiway cut exactly.
  • Pughe-Sanford et al. They propose that sensory neurons learn membership of finite-time coherent sets of their input's dynamics. A balanced coherent pair is a present region and a future region that trajectories move between with high probability over a horizon $\tau$; relaxing the indicators gives singular-function pairs of the stochastic Koopman operator, and a neuron computes one of them, thresholded or rectified. For stable Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics the leading non-constant singular-function pair is linear (higher ones are polynomial), and the linear-feature Galerkin estimator on lag vectors reduces to past–future canonical correlation. Their operator is the stochastic Koopman operator of real input dynamics, not an average of constructed smoothers, and they model biological sensory computation without claiming that trained artificial units do this.

What is unusual about this approach

  • Label-free. Nothing semantic defines the kernel or the basis; labels balance the probe, and after that they only colour and check.
  • Each layer in its own geometry. The construction fits no map between layers or checkpoints (the 2-d layouts are aligned for display only).
  • Depth and training time are one object. A block is a run of snapshots along either axis.
  • Groups of images, not directions. The sparsity is over images; nothing assumes sparse activations.
  • Nothing trained on top. Only fixed computations and eigenproblems.
  • Architecture-agnostic. It needs only activations on a fixed probe set.

The procedure in five steps

Five fixed computations, from activations to sets. Pictures from one example (layer block b7 to pool, step {{fact:final_step}}); exact versions in Method.

1. Compare images within one snapshot

For one snapshot (one site at one checkpoint), centre the activations over the probe images, scale each image's vector to unit length, and take the cosine between every pair of images.

The maths

With $h_i$ the activation of image $i$ in snapshot $c$ and $\bar h$ its mean over the probe, $$ \hat h_i = \frac{h_i - \bar h}{\lVert h_i - \bar h \rVert}, \qquad C_{ij} = \langle \hat h_i, \hat h_j \rangle, \qquad d_{ij} = 1 - C_{ij}. $$ The dot product on each snapshot is the only geometric assumption. Cosine drops the activation norm.

2. Turn similarity into a walk

A kernel rule turns the cosines into neighbourhood weights $K_c$ (default: the {{fact:default_mode}}), and Sinkhorn scaling makes them a symmetric random walk $P_c$ between similar images. Symmetric scaling gives every image the same stationary weight, so differences between snapshots' stationary measures do not enter the block comparison. The lazy walk $M_c = \tfrac12(I + P_c)$ stays put half the time.

The maths

Sinkhorn finds $v > 0$ with $P_c = \mathrm{diag}(v)\, K_c\, \mathrm{diag}(v)$ having every row and column sum equal to 1. For a function $f$ on the images (entry $f_i$ for image $i$), the walk defines a penalty

$$ f^\top (I - P_c)\, f \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} (P_c)_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2 , $$

which is small when $f$ is nearly constant on images the snapshot treats as neighbours. $M_c$ has the eigenvectors of $P_c$, with eigenvalues $\tfrac12(1 + \lambda) \in [0, 1]$. The kernel rule mattered more than anything else here; choosing the kernel explains why the default adapts to each image.

3. Average over a block

A block is a run of snapshots: consecutive sites at one checkpoint (a layer block) or consecutive checkpoints at one site (a time block). Its operator $\bar M$ is the plain average of the snapshots' lazy walks.

The maths

$\bar M = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{c \in B} M_c = \tfrac12(I + \bar P)$, with $\bar P$ the mean walk. The penalty is linear in the walk, so

$$ f^\top (I - \bar P)\, f \;=\; \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{c \in B} f^\top (I - P_c)\, f . $$

This is Froyland's dynamic Laplacian, and Banisch and Koltai's space-time diffusion maps, with depth or training in place of time. The product $M_b \cdots M_a$ is the theory's natural object; the average is what I use, and the two agree closely here (average versus product). Checkpoints count equally, and they are roughly geometric in steps.

4. Take its eigenfunctions, with their retention in every snapshot

The leading eigenvectors $\psi_1, \psi_2, \dots$ of $\bar M$ are the functions on the images with the smallest average variation across the block's walks. An average is not "in every snapshot": a function can rank high while varying a lot in one snapshot. So I always show, next to each eigenfunction, its retention $\rho_c(\psi_k)$ in each snapshot, which marks the snapshots where that candidate distinction is weaker. Retention 1 means the function is constant on every neighbourhood of the snapshot; a random function gets about 0.5. Retention near 1 means a step of the walk changes the function's value little relative to its overall size; lower retention means larger changes.

The maths

Among unit functions orthogonal to the constant $\psi_0$ and to each other, the eigenvectors of $\bar M$ in decreasing order of eigenvalue minimise the block-average penalty (2). Retention in any snapshot $c$, inside or outside the block, is $$ \rho_c(f) = \frac{f^\top M_c f}{f^\top f} \in [0, 1], \qquad \lambda_k = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{c \in B} \rho_c(\psi_k). $$ An individual $\psi_k$ is meaningful only when $\lambda_k$ is separated from its neighbours; otherwise only the span is.

5. Turn the span into sets with SEBA

$r$ is the number of groups you ask for. SEBA takes the constant $\psi_0$ and the leading $r - 1$ non-constant eigenfunctions, and returns $r$ sparse vectors that approximate their span. When the retained eigenvalues are near 1, functions in that span vary little on average across the block's walks; a sparse one is large on some images and zero on the rest, which is what a group looks like. Giving each image to the vector that is largest there yields $r$ candidate coherent sets; images where all $r$ vectors are zero are unassigned.

The maths

SEBA (Froyland, Rock and Sakellariou 2019) alternates two moves on $V = [\psi_0, \dots, \psi_{r-1}]$: rotate inside the span, $Z = VR$, then soft-threshold each column of $Z$ at $\mu = 0.99/\sqrt N$ and renormalise, giving $S$; the next rotation $R$ is the orthogonal polar factor of $V^\top S$. The thresholded $S$ generally leaves the span, so the SEBA vectors are sparse approximations to it, not elements of it. Each column is then sign-fixed, scaled to a maximum of 1, ordered by decreasing minimum entry ("reliability", which numbers the sets) and clipped at 0. Image $i$ goes to $\arg\max_j S_{ij}$ if that maximum is positive, and is unassigned otherwise. How sharply $r$ cuts the spectrum is the gap $\lambda_{r-1} - \lambda_r$. Details in Method.

Vocabulary (glossary): probe image, site, checkpoint, snapshot (a site at a checkpoint), block (a run of snapshots along depth or training), single-snapshot block, eigenfunction $\psi_k$ of the block operator, retention $\rho_c$, coherent set, unassigned.
