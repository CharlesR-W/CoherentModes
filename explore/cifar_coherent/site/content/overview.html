I fed a small image classifier (a ResNet-20 trained on CIFAR-10) the same $N = 2{,}000$ test images at eight saved points during training and recorded its activations at twelve places, from the first convolution to the logits. The question: which groups of images does the network treat alike, at which layers, and since when?

Status

An exploratory side study: one architecture, one training seed, 2,000 probe images. Nothing here is a validated result. Class labels are used only to draw a class-balanced probe (200 images per class); after that they never define a kernel or a basis, and only colour plots and serve as a check.

Things the network treats similarly

By a coherent set I mean a group of images that, in each of a chosen range of layers (or checkpoints), has activations similar to one another and different from the rest. "Similar" is judged in each layer's own geometry; nothing compares positions across layers. The coloured sets on this site are computed candidates for such groups. This is my small, unassuming step towards finding the network's ontology.

A cell is one checkpoint at one site (stem, residual blocks b1 to b9, pool, logits). In each cell I centre the activations over the images, scale each image's vector to unit length, and let $\cos_{ij}$ be the dot product of images $i$ and $j$. A kernel turns cosines into weights, e.g. $K_{ij} = \exp\big(\kappa(\cos_{ij} - 1)\big)$ for $i \ne j$, $K_{ii} = 0$, with an inverse temperature $\kappa$ (the "tempered" in the title); the default rule adapts to each image (below). Rescaling rows and columns of $K$ until each sums to one (Sinkhorn scaling) gives a symmetric random walk $P$ between similar images. For a function on the images, a vector $f$ with entry $f_i$ for image $i$, it defines a penalty

$$ f^\top (I - P)\, f \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2 , $$

small when $f$ is nearly constant on images the cell considers similar. Among unit-length functions orthogonal to the constant $\psi_0$ and to each other, the smallest penalties belong to the leading eigenvectors $\psi_1, \psi_2, \dots$ of $P$: ordinary spectral clustering of one layer, whose splits I call static. The explorer uses the walk $M = \tfrac12(I + P)$ ($I$ the identity), which stays put half the time; it has the same eigenvectors, and each eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $P$ becomes $\tfrac12(1 + \lambda) \in [0, 1]$. A procedure called SEBA (below) turns the leading $r$ eigenvectors into $r$ candidate sets.

From one layer to a block

What I care about is which splits persist. Take a block of cells, say sites b5 to pool at the last checkpoint, and average their walks: $\bar P = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{c \in B} P_c$ and $\bar M = \tfrac12(I + \bar P)$. The penalty is linear in the walk, so

$$ f^\top (I - \bar P)\, f \;=\; \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{c \in B} f^\top (I - P_c)\, f , $$

and the leading eigenvectors of $\bar M$ have the smallest average penalty over the block. No map between layers is needed, only each layer's own sense of which images are similar. The idea comes from fluid dynamics, where averaging over time (Froyland's dynamic Laplacian, Banisch and Koltai's space-time diffusion maps) finds coherent sets, blobs of fluid that move together; here time is depth.

Call $\psi_k^\top M_c \psi_k$ the retention of the unit vector $\psi_k$ in cell $c$: between 0 and 1, closer to 1 the less $\psi_k$ varies between images that cell weights as similar. Each block eigenvalue is the average retention over the block, $\lambda_k = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{c \in B} \psi_k^\top M_c \psi_k$, and the explorer draws retention for every cell as a strip. Reading the two together:

  • A split present in one layer only is demoted by the average. A static split of b5 missing from the block's leading directions may have been blurred by later layers or merely outranked; its retention there helps tell which.
  • A leading eigenvalue well separated from the rest (within one kernel rule), with high, even retention across a wide block, is evidence of a distinction carried across those cells.
  • It is an average, not a guarantee: a split expensive in one cell can still rank high. A dip in the strip marks that cell.

Through training

The same average works along the other axis: fix a site and average over checkpoints. The leading eigenvectors are the splits that layer draws, on average, across that stretch of training; high retention throughout is consistent with a distinction kept at those checkpoints, though eight snapshots cannot date its onset. Checkpoints count equally and are roughly geometric in steps (0, 64, 256, ..., 64,000), so early training weighs heavily.

Spectral clustering, Hankel, and the title

One cell is spectral clustering; a block is spectral clustering of several layers' walks at once. The cross-Hankel in the title is an analogy: a Hankel matrix links a time series' past to its future, and the nearest cross-layer object is the product $Q = M_b \cdots M_a$ ("diffuse at layer $a$, then the next, ..."), which the project's theory builds on. The explorer uses the average instead; their leading spans agree closely here, a median mean $\cos^2$ of 0.99 over layer pairs at the last checkpoint (lowest 0.84; default rule, $r = 3$) (details). Hence "Not-Quite".

Choosing the kernel, and a finding about it

Nothing in the network fixes the kernel, and here it mattered more than anything else. In the trained middle layers (b1 to b8, last checkpoint) the median cosine distance from an image to its 15th nearest neighbour, $d_{15}$, is 0.81 to 0.86, against 1.00 between random images: even near neighbours are not near. I compared 19 kernel rules on 15 real cells with label-free diagnostics: $1 - \lambda_1$ for $M$ (lower means a slower leading mode), the smallest SEBA set, and the participation number $1/\sum_i \psi(i)^4$ of a unit eigenvector ($N$ when all entries are equal in size, 2 when two carry everything).

  • One $\kappa$ per cell fails in the middle layers, in two ways. kNN scale, $\kappa = 1/d_{15}$ (kernel $e^{-1}$ at the median 15th-neighbour distance), gives $\kappa \approx 1.2$: a nearly flat kernel with spread but weak modes (for $\kappa$ from 2 to 5, $1 - \lambda_1$ = 0.39 to 0.47 in b2, b5, b8), to first order the principal components. Half-mass picks $\kappa$ (26 to 43 there) so the 15 nearest neighbours carry half of the median image's kernel weight; then a few unusually close pairs outweigh everything, after Sinkhorn each such image sends nearly all its probability to its partner, and $\psi_1$ sits on one pair (participation 2 from $\kappa \approx 12$ up in b5 and b8). No single $\kappa$ in between is both spread and slow.
  • Rules that adapt to each image worked here. A 15-nearest-neighbour graph (weight 1 if either image is among the other's 15 nearest, else almost 0) and t-SNE-style affinities with a per-image $\kappa_i$ giving each image an effective 100 or 300 neighbours ("perplexity") give every image a comparable neighbourhood, so a single pair is much less likely to take over. In every final cell tested $\psi_1$ is spread (participation 890 to 1,450) and no SEBA set has fewer than 86 images; in the middle layers $1 - \lambda_1$ is 0.05 to 0.17.

So the default is the kNN graph; perplexity 100 and 300 are offered too, and kNN scale and half-mass stay selectable so you can watch them fail. (Per-image rules are not immune: perplexity 15 and 30 still produced 2-image sets in some tested cells.) At pool and logits, where images are far more clustered, every rule gives spread modes. Labels played no part in the choice; checked afterwards, the per-image rules' sets also agree best with the classes in the middle layers (Method). This is a finding about this construction on this network, not a general law.

Reading the plots

The 2-d pictures are UMAP layouts of each cell's activations. Within a checkpoint the twelve sites are embedded jointly (AlignedUMAP, which shapes the layouts); across checkpoints they are only centred, scaled, rotated or reflected and rescaled. Nothing is computed from them; a set that looks scattered can be compact in the operator, or the reverse.

Colours are computed from the selected block only and painted on every cell, so you can see where that block's groups sit in other layers and at other times.

  • Eigenfunction $\psi_k$: blue negative, grey near zero, red positive, scaled by $\max_i |\psi_k(i)|$. The sign is a convention (largest-magnitude entry positive). Nodal domains: the sign of $\psi_k$ alone.
  • SEBA sets (Froyland, Rock and Sakellariou 2019). SEBA seeks $r$ sparse vectors that approximate the span of $\psi_0, \dots, \psi_{r-1}$, each concentrated on a group: it rotates the span and soft-thresholds the rotated vectors at $0.99/\sqrt{N}$, the size of an evenly spread unit vector, and repeats (the thresholded vectors are close to, but generally not in, the span) (details). The vectors are scaled to a maximum of 1 and numbered by reliability; each image takes the colour of the vector that is largest there, set $j$ the $j$-th set colour ( set 1, set 2, ...), and images where all $r$ are zero are grey, "unassigned". The sets depend on $r$; within a group of nearly equal eigenvalues only the span, which SEBA uses, is robust.

Extras. "Layout from" can place the points by a second ResNet-20 (same recipe, another seed; only its UMAP layouts exist) while colours still come from the first: a set that stays in one piece there hints, without showing, that the grouping is not seed-specific. Two more colourings, for curiosity: each image's input-Jacobian norm and the cosine between its loss gradient and the average gradient. None of these enters the kernel or the sets.

What this does not show

  • One model, one seed, one probe sample; no resampling check of the sets.
  • Cosine on centred activations discards activation norms, and the kernel rule, chosen on this network, changes the answer.
  • The block operator is an average, not the theory's product; high rank does not mean "present in every layer". Sets that match classes are an observation, not something the method was told.
