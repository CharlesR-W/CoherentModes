In the tested ResNets, do different seeds and depths go through matching sequences of operators?

Tested: ResNet-20, ResNet-32 and ResNet-56, two training seeds each ({{fact:x.across-nets.n_nets}} networks, one recipe), the {{fact:al.n_images}}-image probe, the eight checkpoints (steps {{fact:al.steps}}), kernel rule {{fact:x.across-nets.rule}}, single-site operators, cuts $k = 3$ and $k = 9$; the other kernel rule under Caveats.

Why it is interesting

The method turns every site of a network (the stem, each residual block's output, the pooled features, the logits) into an operator on the same probe images: a walk that says which images that site treats as similar. Going through a network is then a sequence of operators, one per site. Two networks trained from different random seeds, or ResNet-20 and ResNet-56, have different weights and, for different depths, different numbers of sites. They can still pass the images through the same sequence of operators, with the deeper network taking more sites for each step of it.

If they do, that sequence is reproducible across the tested runs (one recipe, one probe, one architecture family) rather than a property of one run, and the operators of one network describe the others too. Where they do not, individual runs have freedom: two equally accurate networks can organise the images differently along depth. This experiment measures how closely the tested networks' sequences match, against how closely two seeds of the same network match and against what matching a scrambled order of sites would give.

How I measure it

Distance between two snapshots. A snapshot is one site of one network at one checkpoint; its operator is the explorer's lazy walk $M = (I+P)/2$ (Method). Every network sees the same probe images, so two operators can be compared directly, with no matching of images. The projector distance $d_k(A,B) = 1 - \|U_k(A)^\top U_k(B)\|_F^2 / k$ compares the spans of the two operators' leading $k$ eigenfunctions after the constant one: it is zero when the spans are equal and close to one for unrelated spans (definitions in Appendix C).

Matching two sequences. For two networks at one checkpoint, $D$ is the site × site matrix of $d_k$. Dynamic time warping (DTW) finds the monotone path from (stem, stem) to (logits, logits) that minimises the summed distance along it, allowing one site of one network to match several consecutive sites of the other. The DTW cost is that minimum divided by the total step weight, the same for every path, so it is the average distance along the best monotone path. A low cost is a low average distance along that path; it does not establish that every site has a close counterpart, since a few poorly matched sites can sit under a low average. DTW is a comparison tool used on these distances, not part of the construction.

Is the order found or imposed? DTW returns a monotone path whatever the matrix. Beside it I take, for every site, its nearest site in the other network with no order constraint; the inversion fraction is the share of site pairs whose nearest matches come in strictly the opposite order. Repeated matches are allowed and do not count as inversions (all sites matched to one site would score zero), so I also report how many distinct sites the nearest matches reach. Few strict reversals together with many distinct targets mean the unconstrained matches themselves run in order.

Which cut, and how reliable it is. A leading-$k$ span is only well defined when the eigenvalues $k$ and $k+1$ are separated. For every snapshot the operator was rebuilt on two overlapping two-thirds subsets of the images (no labels used) and $\mathrm{span}_k$ is the smallest $\cos^2$ principal angle between the two rebuilds' leading-$k$ spans; the cut is unresolved for a pair of snapshots when $\mathrm{span}_k < 0.5$ in either. At an unresolved cut, $d_k$ depends on how near-equal modes happen to be split. Every DTW cost below is given with the share of its path whose cut is unresolved. I report two cuts. $k = 3$ is the largest cut in the grid resolved in at least nine tenths of the {{fact:x.across-nets.n_final_snapshots}} final-checkpoint snapshots ({{fact:x.across-nets.resolved_share.k3}}). $k = 9$ is the cut the alignment figures open at; it is resolved at pool and logits in every network, but in only {{fact:x.across-nets.resolved_share.k9}} of all final snapshots. The diffusion-kernel distance $h_{16}$, which weights every mode by $\lambda^{16}$ instead of cutting, is the cut-free check.

Which pairs. Two seeds of one depth form a pair of their own (three pairs). Across depth, each depth pair has four seed pairings; summaries use the two different-seed pairings only, because networks trained with the same seed draw the initial weights of the stem and first blocks identically (six pairings in all). Same-seed pairings are in Appendix D.

Controls and nulls

  • Block-permutation null. The optimal DTW cost recomputed after randomly permuting the residual-block sites of one network, with stem, pool and logits fixed ({{fact:al.n_perm}} permutations). It keeps every distance value and the fixed endpoints and removes the order of the blocks. A cost below the null's fifth percentile shows an advantage over globally shuffled blocks. Because the shuffle also moves blocks between stages, it does not by itself separate the order of the stages from a finer correspondence within each stage, and it does not show that individual sites correspond one to one.
  • Stage-preserving shuffle. The same re-optimisation after shuffling the residual blocks of the deeper network only within their own stage ({{fact:x.across-nets.n_stage_perm}} draws, computed for this experiment from the stored distance matrices, cross-depth pairings at the final checkpoint). It keeps which stage every block is in and removes the order of blocks inside a stage, so a cost below it shows that the within-stage order matters too.
  • Seed floor. The DTW cost between the two seeds of one depth at the same checkpoint. A cross-depth cost at the floor means that changing depth moves the sequence about as much as changing the seed does. With two seeds per depth the floor is one value, not a distribution.
  • Constant-matrix reference. On a matrix whose every entry is the mean of $D$, every path costs that mean. It says how much of the cost any path would get.
  • Untrained reference. The same comparison at the first checkpoint, before any training, so that order produced by the architecture before any training is not attributed to learning.
  • The CKA distance on the centred-cosine Grams is a standard baseline, not an output of the method; it is in Appendix D.

Further evidence

Does the answer depend on the small cut? A cut of three modes compares only the coarsest part of each operator, and the match could be weaker in more modes. At $k = 9$ the cost across depth is at most {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.depth.ratio_max}} times the larger seed floor, but most of each path crosses an unresolved cut, so these are sensitivity values. The cut-free diffusion-kernel distance $h_{16}$ gives {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.depth.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.depth.cost_hi}} across depth against seed floors of {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.seed.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.seed.cost_hi}} and null fifth percentiles of {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.depth.null05_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.depth.null05_hi}} (at most {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.depth.ratio_max}} times the larger floor), below the null in every comparison from step {{fact:x.across-nets.h16.below_from.depth_max}} on.

Is the order imposed by DTW? A monotone path exists for any matrix, so a low cost alone could come from DTW forcing an order onto distances that have none. The unconstrained nearest matches give an order check that does not assume monotonicity: at the final checkpoint their inversion fraction (strict reversals only) is at most {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.inv_hi}} at $k = 3$ and {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.depth.inv_hi}} at $k = 9$ in every cross-depth pairing. The shallower network's sites have nearest matches on distinct sites of the deeper one for {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.match.ab_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.match.ab_hi}} of them at $k = 3$ ({{fact:x.across-nets.k9.match.ab_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.match.ab_hi}} at $k = 9$), and the deeper network's nearest matches reach {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.match.ba_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.match.ba_hi}} of the shallower network's sites ({{fact:x.across-nets.k9.match.ba_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.match.ba_hi}}). Against the stage-preserving shuffle, the DTW cost is below the fifth percentile ({{fact:x.across-nets.k3.stage_null.null05_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.stage_null.null05_hi}} at $k = 3$, {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.stage_null.null05_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.stage_null.null05_hi}} at $k = 9$) in {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.stage_null.n_below}} of the {{fact:x.across-nets.n_cross_diff}} pairings at $k = 3$ and {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.stage_null.n_below}} at $k = 9$, and no draw reaches the observed cost: the order of blocks within each stage contributes to the match, not only the order of the stages. The DTW cost is well below the constant-matrix reference ({{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.const_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.const_hi}} at $k = 3$). The path passes through all three stage ends (the last block of each stage) in {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.n_all_ends}} of the {{fact:x.across-nets.n_cross_diff}} pairings at $k = 3$ ({{fact:x.across-nets.k9.depth.n_all_ends}} at $k = 9$) and through at least {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.ends_lo}} in each; its mean gap from the stage-proportional reference is {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.gap_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k3.depth.gap_hi}} stage units at $k = 3$ (zero would be a match proportional to block index within every stage).

Caveats

  • Two seeds per depth: each seed floor is a single comparison, and the cross-depth costs are compared with it, not with a spread of floors. Three depths of one architecture family, trained with one recipe, on one probe.
  • The DTW cost is a mean along the path. A few sites that match poorly can sit under a low mean; the distance at each point of the path against the floor at the same sites is in Appendix D.
  • At $k = 9$ most of every path, across depth and across seeds alike ({{fact:x.across-nets.k9.seed.unres_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.k9.seed.unres_hi}} for the seed pairs), crosses an unresolved cut; those costs measure how sensitive the comparison is, not a property of the sites. At $k = 3$ every cut up to three is resolved in at least {{fact:x.across-nets.resolved_share.k_lo_min}} of the final snapshots, but three modes are a coarse summary of each operator.
  • Kernel rule {{fact:x.across-nets.rule1}}: at the final checkpoint, $k = 3$, the cost across depth is {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.cost_hi}} against seed floors of {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.seed.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.seed.cost_hi}} and null fifth percentiles of {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.null05_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.null05_hi}} ({{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.unres_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.k3.depth.unres_hi}} unresolved). This rule could not be built for {{fact:al.missing.rule1.snapshots}} early ResNet-56 snapshots, so {{fact:x.across-nets.r1.n_missing_cross}} cross-network comparisons are missing under it.
  • Only single-site operators are compared; blocks of several sites are not.
  • The CKA baseline gives costs of {{fact:x.across-nets.cka.depth.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.cka.depth.cost_hi}} across depth against floors of {{fact:x.across-nets.cka.seed.cost_lo}} to {{fact:x.across-nets.cka.seed.cost_hi}}, with inversion fraction at most {{fact:x.across-nets.cka.depth.inv_hi}}: a standard similarity measure also finds an ordered match, so its existence is not specific to the spectral construction.
