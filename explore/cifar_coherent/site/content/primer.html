The question

Which inputs does a network treat the same, at which layer, and since when in training? I ask this without labels: only the network's own activations go in.

Activation cells

  • Stefan Heimersheim and collaborators observed that a layer's activation space behaves as if tiled into Voronoi-like cells.
  • If the rest of the network computes nearly the same thing everywhere in a cell, which cell an input lands in carries most of what the rest of the network uses.
  • At that layer the network then behaves like a machine with discrete states, which could in principle be read off.

The same problem in fluids: coherent structures

Fluid dynamics has the same question. A flow stirs everything, and you want to know which parcels of fluid move together and where the boundaries between them lie. Cell walls play the part that Lagrangian coherent structures (Haller and collaborators) play in a flow.

  • Walls are ridges of stretching. The finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) at a point is the rate at which two nearby particles there separate over a time window. Where it peaks along a ridge, fluid on one side and fluid on the other end up far apart.
  • Depth plays time. The rest of the network is the flow map from layer ℓ to the output. Its FTLE at an activation $x$ is $\frac{1}{h}\log\sigma_{\max}(J)$, where $J$ is the Jacobian of the layers after ℓ at $x$ and $h$ is how many there are. Inside a cell the downstream map is nearly flat, so neighbours stay together. At a wall a small step sends the output somewhere else.
  • Which ridges. Cell walls are ridges of the forward-time FTLE seen at layer ℓ, which Haller calls repelling structures: they divide inputs by where the network sends them next. Backward-time ridges are the attracting structures, the places in a later layer onto which the network gathers its inputs.
  • Where this comes from. In earlier work (the CellsDevelopmental project) I measured this quantity directly: how far a perturbation at one layer grows through the layers after it, a per-layer FTLE, alongside the activation plateaus behind the cell picture.

Fluid dynamics also met the obstacle described in the next section. An FTLE field needs the velocity field and a dense grid of particles, differentiated point by point; an ocean gives you a few hundred drifting floats. The field moved to data-driven methods that take sparse particle trajectories and ask which particles stay together, not where the walls are: transfer operators and coherent sets (Froyland and collaborators), clustering of sparse trajectories (Froyland and Padberg-Gehle 2015), spectral clustering of trajectories (Hadjighasem et al. 2016), and space-time diffusion maps (Banisch and Koltai 2017). Hadjighasem et al. (2017) compare these methods with the FTLE-based ones on the same flows.

This method follows the same move. Each probe image is a Lagrangian particle: a drifter whose trajectory is its activation at each layer (or at each training checkpoint). The rest of the primer builds the coherent-set construction from these trajectories.

Takeaway: cell walls are where the rest of the network pulls neighbouring inputs apart, as FTLE ridges are where a flow pulls neighbouring fluid apart. Probe images are the drifters.

Find the groups, not the walls

  • Measuring boundaries directly does not scale, and the data only sample the space, so most of any boundary is never seen. This is the FTLE route, and it has the FTLE route's cost.
  • Instead, as in the fluid methods above, I ask which inputs the network treats as interchangeable. A cell is then a group of inputs that few neighbourhood links leave.
  • Finding such groups is what spectral clustering does: one similarity matrix per layer, one forward pass per input and checkpoint, no Jacobians.
  • The number of probes is mine to choose, and the cost grows with it, not with the size of the network. That is what makes the problem tractable.
  • It also sets the scale at which I look. A neighbour graph on a few thousand images cannot resolve a cell that holds only a handful of them, so the probe set works as a coarse-graining. This helps: a layer may well have far more cells than anyone could characterise, and even a complete map of them would be too fine to interpret. At the probe scale the method reports the coarse structure first; more probes resolve finer structure where it is wanted.
Takeaway: instead of mapping where the network's behaviour changes, I look for groups of inputs that neighbourhood links rarely leave. The number of probes sets both the cost and the scale of the groups, so the coarse structure comes first.
The cell picture, the fluid analogy, and the working hypothesis that inside a cell the downstream output is close to the average over nearby inputs (a limited form of harmonic label propagation), are motivation. The analogy is loose in known places: the layers of a network are not an invertible flow, the activation space changes dimension from layer to layer, and a ReLU network's Jacobian is piecewise constant, so its walls are steep jumps rather than smooth ridges. Whether measured FTLE ridges coincide with the boundaries of the sets found here has not been tested. A first labelled test of the cell picture has been designed but not yet run; until it has, this is not a result.

From activations to a neighbour graph

At one layer of one checkpoint, each probe image is a point: its activation vector. I link each image to the images it is most similar to, measured by cosine similarity (activations centred, then scaled to unit length). The dot product is the only geometric assumption.

What the site actually uses instead of plain "nearest neighbours"

The links are weighted by a kernel $K_{ij}$ of the cosine distance, with no self-links. The default rule gives every image a comparable neighbourhood (a 15-nearest-neighbour graph or a per-image bandwidth); a single global bandwidth fails in the middle layers. See Per-snapshot operator and Choosing the kernel.

A random walk on the graph

"The layer treats these images the same" becomes: the walk mixes quickly among them and leaks out slowly.

$$ P = \mathrm{diag}(v)\,K\,\mathrm{diag}(v), \qquad \textstyle\sum_j P_{ij} = \sum_i P_{ij} = 1 $$

The textbook walk is $D^{-1}K$ (divide each row by its total). I instead rescale rows and columns together (Sinkhorn) so every row and every column sums to 1; then every layer and checkpoint has the same uniform "resting" distribution, which is what lets them be averaged later.

Slowest modes: Laplacian eigenfunctions

An eigenfunction assigns a number to every image. The slowest ones are the smoothest assignments: linked images get similar numbers, so the value can only change where links are thin.

$$ \tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2 \;\text{ small}, \qquad P\,\psi_k = \lambda_k\,\psi_k $$

Why this is principled, and how many groups

  • Nothing is trained and no labels are used. Every step is a similarity, a rescaling and an eigendecomposition; class labels are only used afterwards, to check the groups.
  • Standard mathematics. This is spectral clustering and diffusion maps (Shi and Malik; Ng, Jordan and Weiss; Coifman and Lafon), with decades of theory behind it. Once activations are collected and compared, the eigenproblem has one row per probe image, not per neuron or parameter.
  • Two main resolution controls: the neighbourhood scale (the kernel rule; it matters) and the number of groups I ask for. Several slow non-constant modes suggest candidate groups, and a gap helps choose how many; it does not dictate a unique answer.
One more detail: the lazy walk

The site uses $M = \tfrac12(I + P)$, a walk that stays put half the time. It has the same eigenvectors as $P$ and eigenvalues $\tfrac12(1 + \lambda)$ in $[0, 1]$, so a graph that is nearly two-coloured (eigenvalue near $-1$) is not mistaken for a slow mode.

One layer is a snapshot; the groups that matter persist

I average the walks over a run of snapshots: neighbouring layers (a layer block) or neighbouring training checkpoints (a time block). Depth or training plays the role of time:

$$ \bar P = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{c \in B} P_c . $$

Groups that stay together across the whole run are coherent sets, the fluid-dynamics idea from earlier: blobs of fluid that hold together while the flow stirs everything around them. An average can hide a weak snapshot, so each set is also checked snapshot by snapshot (retention, next slide). No map between layers or checkpoints is fitted; each snapshot keeps its own geometry.

From eigenvectors to sets

  • SEBA (Froyland, Rock and Sakellariou) turns the leading $r$ eigenvectors into $r$ sparse vectors, one per set.
  • Each image joins the set whose vector is largest for it; images with no clearly positive vector stay unassigned (grey). Nothing forces every image into a group.
  • Retention asks, snapshot by snapshot, how much of a set the walk keeps inside the set after one step: high means the set holds together there.

What is assumed, and what is not

Assumed or chosenNot used
The dot product on activations (cosine, which drops the norm)No training on top of the network: only fixed computations and eigenproblems

How I check it. Nulls that keep part of the structure and break the rest (shuffling which image is which across snapshots; a Gaussian surrogate matched to each snapshot's covariance spectrum; SEBA on random subspaces); baselines (the method on pixels, on the untrained network, and plain PCA + k-means); and a second training seed. See Appendix B: Baselines and nulls, the Method, or try it in the Explorer.

Coherent sets are groups of images that one snapshot's neighbourhoods keep together, and that keep holding together across a run of layers or of training; they come from activations, cosine and eigenvectors alone, with labels used only to check them afterwards.

This is exploratory, on one small network and one probe set. The average operator and its spectrum are well defined once the kernel and block are chosen, but individual eigenvectors are only meaningful when their eigenvalues are separated, the sets depend on where $r$ cuts the spectrum, and the sites, centring, cosine, kernel rule and equal weights could all have been chosen otherwise.
